Insights.

Chapter 4’s image of looking down on fields from a plane has become a metaphor for reviewing design projects. Instead of staying zoomed into pixels and type sizes, it helps to mentally “fly up” and ask: what does the whole landscape look like, and where are the paths, gaps, and clusters? This higher view makes it easier to improvise within a project, noticing how pages, interactions, and moments connect into something coherent.

The Work Out

Design rarely happens just because the cursor is blinking on a screen. When the mind feels stuck, moving the body going for a walk, doodling loose lines, pacing around the room often shakes something free and gently guides ideas into focus. This has changed how studio time feels: not as a rigid desk-bound grind, but as a rhythm of wandering, observing, and then returning to shape what appears.

Drawing As Thinking

Loose, repetitive marks have become a small ritual before any serious piece of work. The point is not to make something pretty, but to move enough that the knots in thinking start to loosen and patterns slowly surface. Treating these scribbles as “kinetic, not aesthetic” takes the pressure off perfection and makes space for accidents to reveal unexpected directions.

Message, Tone, and Form

One of the most practical lessons for these reviews is to check every piece of work against three simple questions:

-What is the message?
-What tone carries that message?
-What form does it take in the world?

When a project feels off, it is usually because one of these three has drifted away from the others—perhaps the message is clear but the tone is too stiff, or the format fights against the story being told. Bringing them back into alignment turns scattered experiments into a design that feels inevitable and strangely “magical,” even though it grew from many small, human decisions.
